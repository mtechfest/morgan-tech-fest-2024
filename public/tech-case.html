AI for Small Business

The Workflow Adoption Challenge. Help a business with fewer than 20 employees adopt AI in a way that fits how it actually works.

The challenge

One Question, Three Sectors

How might a business with fewer than 20 employees adopt AI in a way that fits its actual workflow, creates measurable value, and can be sustained without a technical team?

Competition definition: a small business has fewer than 20 employees

Student teams of 2–4 pick one of three composite small-business cases, map how the work happens today, find the highest-value intervention point, and pitch an AI-enabled redesign the business could realistically adopt. No prototype required. Finalists pitch live on September 19.

The case

The Gap Is Not Access. It Is Adoption.

AI tools are widely available, but the smallest firms adopt them at a lower rate. Many owners do not see how AI applies to their day-to-day work, lack implementation knowledge, or have legitimate privacy and security concerns.

Adoption by business size

  • <20% of firms with four or fewer employees reported using AI
  • 37% of firms with at least 250 employees reported using AI

Why firms stay out

  • 65% of near-term non-adopters said AI was not applicable
  • 22% cited limited knowledge of AI capabilities
  • 20% cited privacy or security concerns

Shallow adoption

  • 57% use AI in three or fewer business functions
  • 64% made no organizational adjustments for AI
  • 15% trained staff or developed new workflows

Business pressure already exists

  • 75% face rising costs of goods, services, or wages
  • 57% struggle to reach customers and grow sales
  • 56% struggle to pay operating expenses
  • 51% manage uneven cash flow

Sources: U.S. Census Bureau Business Trends and Outlook Survey (Dec 2025–May 2026) and AI Supplement (Nov 2025–Jan 2026); Federal Reserve Banks 2025 Report on Employer Firms. Full list below.

A useful AI solution starts with an operating problem, not a tool. The statistics establish the opportunity. Your team must translate it into a credible workflow and adoption plan.
Workflow first

Understand the Work Before Proposing AI

Adoption fails when a solution ignores who does the work, when decisions happen, where information lives, and what breaks under pressure. Begin with the current operating flow, not an AI feature.

The required discovery path

  1. Team. Who owns each task?
  2. Flow. What happens, and when?
  3. Friction. Where does work stall?
  4. AI role. What should change?
  5. Outcome. How will value show up?
The scenarios below are realistic composites grounded in public operating guidance; staffing counts are case assumptions. You may validate or refine the flow through interviews, observation, or credible sources, but you must label every change you make. The goal is not to automate the whole business. Find the best intervention point.
Choose one sector

Three Composite Cases

Each business has fewer than 20 employees and no full-time technical team. Open a case for the full brief.

Sector 1 Independent Consumer Business 8 employees · no full-time technical staff Orders or bookings, capacity, production or service, fulfillment, and repeat sales. Open the case ↓Close the case ↑

An owner-operated business selling food, products, or personal services directly to customers, such as a small restaurant, home bakery, salon, candle maker, or specialty retailer.

The team
  • 1 owner-operator
  • 1 customer and order coordinator
  • 4 production or service staff
  • 1 fulfillment or front-counter staff member
  • 1 part-time administrator
A typical operating flow
  1. Publish the offer and availability
  2. Receive an order, booking, or walk-in
  3. Check inventory and capacity
  4. Schedule labor and materials
  5. Make the product or deliver the service
  6. Fulfill the order and collect payment
  7. Update stock and financial records
  8. Follow up and plan the next cycle
Data and tools
  • Orders, bookings, and point-of-sale records
  • Calls, texts, and social-media direct messages
  • Customer history and preferences
  • Inventory, ingredients, and materials
  • Staff schedule and sales data
Operational friction
  • Requests arrive through many channels
  • Demand is difficult to predict
  • Capacity and stock can become misaligned
  • Reconciliation is manual
  • Owner administration spills into after-hours work
Design guardrails
  • Choose one specific business model
  • Preserve customer approval
  • Maintain food-safety controls where applicable
  • Protect customer and payment data
  • Fit a phone-first, small-budget workflow
The design questionWhere can AI reduce coordination, improve capacity decisions, or strengthen repeat sales without disrupting the customer experience?

Discovery requirement: Select one specific business type. Map the handoffs from first customer contact through fulfillment and reconciliation. Do not design one generic solution for every example.

Grounded in U.S. Small Business Administration guidance on customer purchase steps, marketing, and after-sale support, plus U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics descriptions of retail and food-service order, inventory, and payment work.

Sector 2 Specialty-Crop Farm 15 people at peak · no full-time technical staff Field decisions, harvest, packing, fulfillment, and records. Open the case ↓Close the case ↑

The team must coordinate field conditions, labor, harvest timing, and customer demand. Decisions happen outdoors, change quickly, and are recorded across paper, texts, and spreadsheets.

The team
  • 1 owner-operator
  • 1 field manager
  • 5 year-round field and packing staff
  • 7 seasonal workers at peak
  • 1 sales and delivery coordinator
A typical operating flow
  1. Review orders and conditions
  2. Set daily crew priorities
  3. Plant, cultivate, and irrigate
  4. Monitor crops, pests, and weather
  5. Choose what and when to harvest
  6. Wash, grade, and pack
  7. Fulfill and deliver orders
  8. Update yield, labor, and safety records
Data and tools
  • Weather and field observations
  • Crop plans and photos
  • Yield and labor hours
  • Orders and inventory
  • Paper records, texts, and spreadsheets
Operational friction
  • Weather reshapes the day
  • Recordkeeping competes with fieldwork
  • Demand and harvest do not always match
  • Seasonal crews require coordination
  • Connectivity can be unreliable
Design guardrails
  • The farmer verifies agronomic advice
  • Protect food-safety controls
  • Work with incomplete data
  • Support low-connectivity use
  • Account for seasonality
The design questionWhere can AI turn scattered observations into a timely, useful decision without making unsafe agronomic claims?

Discovery requirement: Trace one product from field plan to customer. Identify who creates each record, who needs it next, and where delay, re-entry, or guesswork enters the flow.

Grounded in USDA/NIFA small-farm training and Cornell and Penn State Extension guidance on production, post-harvest handling, and recordkeeping.

Sector 3 Nurse-Led Home-Health Agency 16 employees · no full-time technical staff Intake, care planning, scheduling, visits, documentation, and billing. Open the case ↓Close the case ↑

The agency coordinates clinical care across homes while handling referrals, schedules, documentation, compliance, and billing. A missed handoff can affect both care and payment.

The team
  • 1 nurse-owner or administrator
  • 1 registered-nurse clinical supervisor
  • 1 intake and scheduling coordinator
  • 1 billing and office administrator
  • 12 nurses, therapists, and aides
A typical operating flow
  1. Receive the referral and complete intake
  2. Verify eligibility and needs
  3. Complete the initial assessment
  4. Create or update the plan of care
  5. Match staff and schedule the route
  6. Provide the home visit
  7. Document services and escalate concerns
  8. Review quality, bill, and provide updates
Data and tools
  • Referral and eligibility data
  • Credentials, skills, and availability
  • Patient location and plan of care
  • Visit notes and assessments
  • Timekeeping, claims, and payroll
Operational friction
  • Call-outs disrupt schedules
  • Skill and location matching is manual
  • Notes arrive late or incomplete
  • Data may be entered more than once
  • Families seek timely updates
Design guardrails
  • Protect electronic health data
  • Keep clinical judgment human
  • Use role-based access
  • Create an audit trail
  • Never allow unsupervised diagnosis
The design questionWhere can AI reduce administrative load and improve coordination without making a clinical decision or exposing protected health information?

Discovery requirement: Follow one referral through the first completed visit and billing review. Mark every handoff, duplicate entry, wait state, and decision that requires licensed judgment.

Grounded in CMS home-health guidance on referrals, plans of care, assessment, documentation, and quality, plus HHS HIPAA privacy and security guidance.

The assignment

Redesign One Workflow, Credibly

Choose one of the three sector cases. Diagnose the current flow, select the highest-value intervention point, then design an AI-enabled solution the team could realistically adopt.

Your pitch must show

  1. The current team, workflow, handoffs, and information sources.
  2. A specific bottleneck supported by evidence or labeled assumptions.
  3. The future workflow, AI role, and human decision rights.
  4. An implementation, onboarding, and change-management path.
  5. Cost, measurable outcomes, safeguards, and transferability.

Required constraints

Fewer than 20 employees No full-time technical staff Implementable within 90 days First-year cost clearly disclosed Measurable result within six months Privacy, security, and human oversight
The solution can be a product, service, training model, workflow, or hybrid approach.
Not enough: a generic chatbot, a tool list, or an untested promise of efficiency. AI must change a defined task, handoff, or decision in the mapped workflow.
The pitch

One Structure for Every Team

Every team uses the same workflow-centered evidence path. This keeps the three sectors comparable while leaving room for different solutions.

Six-slide core deck

  1. Business and current flow. Who does the work, and how does it happen today?
  2. Bottleneck and evidence. Where does value leak, and what supports that claim?
  3. Future flow and AI role. What changes, and why is AI the right mechanism?
  4. Experience and adoption. How will staff begin, learn, verify, and sustain use?
  5. Feasibility and economics. What will it cost, require, and take to implement?
  6. Impact, trust, and scale. How will outcomes, risk, and transferability be shown?

Competition format

  • Teams of 2–4 participants.
  • Submit a PDF: cover plus six core slides, with an appendix of up to three slides.
  • Final: 7-minute pitch and 5-minute question-and-answer period.
  • Prototype optional; not required.

Evidence rules

  • Use the supplied data.
  • Cite additional claims.
  • Label assumptions.
  • Disclose AI assistance.
Judges look for a grounded decision, a usable solution, and a credible path from first use to sustained value. Polish cannot substitute for evidence.
Judging

A Rubric Built for Fair Comparison

Judges score the quality of the decision and its implementation path. Presentation matters, but it carries less weight than evidence, value, and feasibility.

20%

Workflow understanding and evidence

Maps roles, sequence, handoffs, and data; uses evidence accurately; separates facts from assumptions.

20%

Business value and adoption

Addresses a meaningful need; fits current behavior; explains onboarding, trust, and sustained use.

20%

Solution and responsible AI

Makes AI’s role necessary and clear; protects privacy; keeps appropriate human judgment.

20%

Feasibility and implementation

Provides realistic cost, resources, timeline, integration, and operating ownership.

10%

Measurement and transferability

Defines baselines and outcomes; shows how the approach could adapt to another small-business context.

10%

Pitch and Q&A

Communicates clearly, prioritizes well, and responds to questions with command of the case.

Tie-breaker: higher Feasibility and Implementation score, then Workflow Understanding and Evidence.
